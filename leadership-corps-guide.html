LEADERSHIP CORPS PREP KIT

What is Leadership Corps?

In shortYou will try every program area at the conference. Get ready first with the Prep Kit.

Leadership Corps is an experience-based introduction to all aspects of state government!

Your mission from now until the conference

Gain an understanding and learn as much as possible about Youth in Government, how the state government operates, and what your next steps are to prepare for the conference. Leadership Corps is a unique experience for all delegates in 8th grade. This is the only program area designed for our youngest delegates at the conference, and the only one where you’ll get to experience each piece throughout the weekend.

Things you will do as a Leadership Corps member
  • Complete the required Prep Kit
  • Meet all required submission deadlines
  • Participate in all tours, trials, and small group sessions
  • Adhere to the Code of Conduct

You will participate in small groups, skill-building, and leadership development activities that will enhance your role as an active citizen; you’ll also make new friends along the way! As a member of the Leadership Corps, you will be introduced to each program area of Youth in Government through interactive activities. “Democracy is not a spectator sport.”

  • Writing and debating bills as a legislator
  • Participating in trial and appellate court cases as an attorney
  • Learning about the media and creating newsworthy stories about your YIG experience as a reporter
  • Voting in elections to select next year’s Youth Officers
This will be your only chance EVER in Youth in Government to experience all the program areas at once; enjoy it! Remember that all the skills that you learn here will help you become an active and engaged citizen and a future YIG delegate. Along the way, you will have a chance to become a better speaker, leader, and citizen. As a bonus, you’ll meet new people from around the state of Minnesota. What more could you ask for?

LAUNCH MATERIALS

Definitions and Terms

In shortLearn the words, list your state issues, and practice speaking.

Read these before you start your interviews. They will help when you start conversations.

Definitions

  • Bills
    A proposed change to state law is called a bill. Bills follow a specific format that fits existing legislation.
  • Law
    A bill that is passed and signed by the executive head of state. A state executive is the Governor. The national executive is the President of the United States. It also can be called a statute.
  • Constituent
    The people politicians have been elected to represent. At YIG, these will be the people you interview with your surveys.
  • Representative
    The person who has been elected to represent their constituents, serving in the House of Representatives or the Senate. At YIG, this will be you!
  • State Law
    A law that only applies to the people within the boundaries of a state, like Minnesota or Wisconsin.
  • Federal Law
    A law that applies to the people within the boundaries of the United States. These laws will supersede state laws if there is a conflict between the two.
  • Lobbyist
    A lobbyist’s job is to ensure that a bill gets passed, defeated, or amended, depending on their client's wishes. Lobbyists are the movers and shakers of the political world. They use their connections and influence to support bills throughout their debate on the House or Senate floor.
  • Floor Debate
    When a bill and the parts of a bill are debated among the members of the House or the Senate. Bills can either be struck down and go no further, amended to change or add things to the bill, or they can also be passed along to the executive head with approval of a majority vote of members.
  • Trial Court
    A court of law where a case is heard for the first time. Witnesses give testimony and a judge or jury decides who is guilty or not guilty — or who is liable or not liable.
  • Appellate Court
    Appellate courts review the procedures and the decisions in the trial court to make sure that the proceedings were fair and that the proper law was applied correctly in the trial court case.

YIG specific terms

  • Delegation
    A group of students who represent their community at the Youth in Government conference. Think about it as the people you came with from your school!
  • Delegation Director
    The individual(s) in charge of your delegation. They are your best resource for general conference questions.
  • State Office
    The group of individuals who work behind the scenes to put on the conference. In January, they can help you with any issues, like nametags. Talk with your delegation director first.
  • Conference
    Also, Youth in Government. The weekend in January when everyone comes together to put what we’ve been working on into action.
  • Program/Program Area
    The specific spot at Youth in Government where you’ve been assigned. Hint: yours is Leadership Corps!
  • Elected Officer
    A delegate who runs for election and is voted into a position by their peers.
  • Appointed Official
    A delegate who is selected by the Officers and State Office to help lead program areas for the next conference.
  • Media
    At YIG, media members have a chance to be involved in the radio, newspaper, TV, or digital media.

Pick any three terms above and explain them in your own words, as if to a friend.

Public speaking tips

When speaking one-on-one (like you will for your Prep Kit interviews) or in front of a small group (like you will at Youth in Government), it’s important to be prepared. Below are a few ideas that will make this preparation easier!

Outline your thoughts

  • Know what you’re going to say before you get up to speak
  • When jotting down notes, use keywords, and not whole sentences.
    • When your lines are written out, you can get stuck looking down at your paper the whole time
  • Be clear and concise. Try not to go on and on; stay focused on your message.
  • Don’t be afraid! You don’t need to know exactly what you’re going to say as you speak, but thinking about your ideas and organizing them certainly helps get your message across clearly.

Confidence is key

  • Feel confident in your presentation!
    • Even if you’re nervous, no one can see your nerves, so they won’t even know.
  • Speak directly to your audience; eye contact is key

Be heard

  • Speak with energy and emotion. Change your tone of voice to illustrate your point.
  • Project your voice and remember to avoid mumbling or talking too fast.

Be respectful

  • Share your opinion without belittling others or their views.
  • If contradicting another view, state the factual reasons why you disagree, not just that you disagree.
    • Think facts vs. feelings: Is this a factual contradiction or a feeling that I just don’t like what they’re saying?
Now it’s your turn to practice! Choose one topic below and be prepared to state your opinion using the following format:

I think/believe that ____ because ____
  • Which is better—gum or mints?
  • Should schools enforce a dress code?
  • Is it important to vote?
  • Should people from other states be able to hunt in your state?

Constituent survey overview

Each Leadership Corps delegate will survey 20 community members using the attached documents, which are also available in YAPP.

Delegates should connect with the community they represent. These constituents may be peers or adults from their school, community, neighborhood, place of worship, or delegation. Students may also talk with family, but family responses should not be included in this project.

Delegates will tally their results, complete the Survey Debrief questions and submit them on Airtable. These responses will not be accepted in paper format. These responses will assist you in drafting your first bill at the Youth in Government conference in January.

Survey directions

  • Review the common definitions, terms, and current state issues (the LAUNCH section above). These sheets may be completed independently if they were not completed at LAUNCH. Keep these sheets as they may be helpful references when starting conversations.
  • Have conversations with peers or adults you know about the topics that they are interested in. Six topics have been identified so we can develop a big-picture look at important issues for the state. Take notes on the survey forms provided (the interviews below).
  • Ask open-ended questions to learn why the issue matters, whom it affects, its scale, and what steps should come next. For example, why is alternative energy important to you? How can Minnesotans take steps to become more energy efficient?
  • Compile information from interviews on the table provided (Tallying the Responses).
  • Complete Survey Debrief questions.
  • Enter all the information from the Tallying and Debrief sections on the online registration system (email from Airtable with the form and a link to submit). These pages will be returned to you at the conference.

    Case summary

    Review the following case summary. Complete the questions to prepare for your mock trial and court of appeals case during Model Assembly.

    Minnesota v. Arnold, 2025

    Acting on information from a confidential informant, police officer Matthew Johnson observed two individuals bagging a white, powdery substance for approximately fifteen (15) minutes. This observation was made through a ground-floor window of an apartment building. Officer Johnson called the police station to request a warrant to search the property based on the “in plain view doctrine.” Before a search warrant could be executed, the individuals left in a vehicle from the apartment. Police subsequently stopped the vehicle and during the stop, saw a handgun and a small, black pouch in the car. The officers arrested Arnold and Brown. A search of the vehicle revealed forty-seven (47) grams of cocaine in sandwich baggies. When a search warrant was executed of the apartment, cocaine residue was found along with additional baggies. During the search it was revealed that Arnold and Brown were not the lease holders [renters] of the apartment, but were using the apartment to package cocaine.

    Prosecutors charged the defendants with conspiracy to commit a controlled substance crime and aiding and abetting in a controlled substance crime. Defendants moved to suppress all evidence obtained from the search of the apartment and car, citing the initial observation violated their Fourth Amendment rights and any evidence obtained thereafter was “fruit from the poisonous tree” [if any primary evidence is illegally obtained, any secondary evidence obtained is inadmissible in court.] The trial court held that the Fourth Amendment protections were not extended to Arnold and Brown as they were not overnight social guests, merely temporary, out-of-state visitors. The court also held that Johnson’s search did not constitute a search under the Fourth Amendment.

    On appeal, the Minnesota Court of Appeals found that Arnold’s claim that he was primarily a social guest was inconsistent with evidence suggesting that the apartment’s primary use was business—to package illegal substances. Because his primary purpose was business, he did not have standing under the Fourth Amendment to object to Johnson’s search. Additionally, the Court affirmed Brown’s conviction.

    This case has now been appealed to the Minnesota Supreme Court. Does the defendant have a reasonable expectation of privacy as a temporary guest? Was Officer Johnson’s search illegal? If so, was any resulting evidence also inadmissible?
